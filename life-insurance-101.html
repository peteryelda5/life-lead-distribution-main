1. Purpose

Welcome to Life Insurance 101 from Vivid Life Advisors. This lesson is for someone starting from zero. Life insurance helps protect people financially when a covered death occurs. The customer pays premiums, and the insurance company agrees to pay a death benefit under the policy's terms. That money may help a family replace income, pay debts, or cover final expenses. Your job is to understand the client's situation and help them find suitable, affordable protection. Start by asking: Who depends on this person? What bills would remain? How long is coverage needed? And what payment can they maintain? A bigger policy is not automatically better. A quote is an estimate. An application is a request. Neither automatically means coverage has started. Keep those distinctions in mind throughout this lesson.

2. Know the people and the words

Let's define the people involved. The carrier is the insurance company. It evaluates applications, issues policies, collects premiums, and evaluates claims. Vivid Life Advisors is an agency, not the insurer. An agent, also called a producer, helps clients understand options and handles authorized sales and service work. The insured is the person whose life is covered. The policyowner has ownership rights. The beneficiary is named to receive the death benefit. The owner, insured, and beneficiary do not have to be the same person. A premium is the payment for the policy. Underwriting is the carrier's process for evaluating risk and deciding eligibility and price. A rider adds or changes benefits. The effective date is when coverage starts under the carrier's requirements. In force means coverage is active according to the insurer. An agency or an insurance marketing organization may support you, but it cannot override the insurer's underwriting decision.

3. Match the product to the need

Term life covers a specified period and generally does not build cash value. It can fit a temporary need, such as protection while children depend on a parent's income. Whole life is permanent coverage with contractual guarantees when required premiums and conditions are met. Distinguish guaranteed values from any nonguaranteed dividends. Universal life is another permanent design, with policy charges and funding requirements that need monitoring. Indexed universal life, or I U L, uses an index-linked crediting formula. It is not direct investment in an index, and illustrated nonguaranteed values are not promises. Final expense and mortgage protection describe needs or marketing categories, not one universal policy. Identify the actual product and its benefit schedule. Simplified issue can still involve health questions and checks. Guaranteed issue does not mean full benefits start immediately for every cause of death. Understand graded benefits, waiting periods, exclusions, and affordability before explaining an option.

4. From lead to active coverage

Here is the process. First, a lead is a potential customer's information, not a sale and not proof of consent to every type of contact. Check notes and contact restrictions. In an authorized conversation, review the person's needs, existing coverage, budget, and time horizon. Use accurate information and current carrier rules to compare eligible options. The applicant reviews their information and completes required consent and signatures through the approved process. After submission, the carrier may request more information, approve the application, change the offer, postpone it, or decline it. Explain the actual offer and complete delivery requirements. Before saying the client is covered, confirm the effective date, payment requirements, and outstanding conditions with the insurer. In our portal, a submitted deal, an agent's pipeline stage, and the carrier's status are different things. Moving a card to in force does not activate insurance. After coverage starts, continue helping the client with policy documents, questions, and authorized service.

5. AP is production—not your paycheck

Now let's separate the client's premium from your compensation. A hundred dollar monthly premium equals twelve hundred dollars in annualized premium, or A P. That is a production measure, not your income. Here is a fictional example. If a contract pays eighty percent of twelve hundred dollars of eligible premium, the first-year commission would be nine hundred sixty dollars. If seventy-five percent of that commission is advanced, the advance would be seven hundred twenty dollars. The remaining two hundred forty dollars depends on the contract's earning and payment rules. These are teaching numbers, not your guaranteed compensation. Actual products and contracts may use different bases, fees, schedules, or exclusions. An advance is money paid before it is fully earned. A chargeback is a contractual recovery of commission, which may happen when coverage ends or premiums are not paid. Renewal commissions and agency overrides follow their own contracts. Track actual deposits, business expenses, and potential chargebacks separately from leaderboard production.

6. Help clients keep suitable coverage

Submitting an application is not the end of your work. Persistency describes how well policies stay active over time. It starts with coverage that fits the client's need and a payment they can maintain. If an initial premium fails, follow the carrier's instructions and contact the client appropriately. Do not assume coverage is active. A grace period is an opportunity defined by the policy and applicable requirements; confirm the actual deadline rather than assuming every carrier uses the same number of days. If the insurer changes the offer, explain the new terms and obtain required acceptance. Never accept on the client's behalf. If coverage lapses or the client cancels, record the insurer's status, help with authorized service requests, and review any commission adjustment. Document the action, who is responsible, and the next follow-up date. Good service does not mean pressuring someone to keep unsuitable coverage. It means helping them make an informed decision and keeping accurate records.

7. Build good habits from day one

A useful daily routine starts with appointments, callbacks, carrier requirements, and urgent service tasks. Prepare with current product materials. Check the lead's notes and contact restrictions. Hold authorized conversations, listen carefully, record accurate outcomes, and schedule the next action. Review your results with your leader to improve your work. Before selling, complete the licensing and carrier authorization requirements for the relevant states and products. In Michigan, selling, soliciting, or negotiating insurance requires the appropriate producer license, and acting for an insurer requires appointment. Training and an N P N alone are not authorization. Never invent health answers, contact details, or consent. Never sign for the applicant. Use approved systems for private client information. Respect a do-not-call request; do not use another duplicate record to work around it. Do not replace existing coverage casually. Review lost benefits, costs, and new limitations through required procedures. When unsure, ask a qualified supervisor before proceeding.

8. Put it together

Let's put it together with a fictional client. The client wants protection for their family, has a limited monthly budget, and already has some coverage. You start with their needs and existing policy, not with a promise of a new sale. A properly authorized agent compares suitable options and explains the actual terms. The client gives accurate answers and completes the application process. The carrier makes its decision. You then verify coverage conditions and help with service. If the client pays seventy-five dollars monthly, the annualized premium is nine hundred dollars. That still does not tell you your commission without the applicable contract. You are ready for the next step when you can explain a premium, a beneficiary, term versus permanent coverage, and why submission is not the same as active coverage. 1. What are we actually helping people do?

Life insurance is a contract with an insurance company. The customer pays premiums. The insurer agrees to pay a death benefit when a covered death occurs, subject to the contract’s terms. That money can help beneficiaries handle lost income, debts and final expenses.

Your job starts with understanding the financial problem: Who depends on this person? What would change if their income stopped? How long is protection needed? What payment can they comfortably maintain?

Protection first. A larger policy is not automatically a better recommendation. Coverage needs, affordability, eligibility and policy terms all matter.

A quote is an estimate. An application is a request. Neither one automatically means the person is insured.

2. Who does what?

Carrier / insurer
The company that underwrites the application, issues the contract, collects premiums and evaluates claims.
Agency
The organization supporting agents with training, operations and carrier relationships. Vivid Life Advisors is an agency, not the insurer.
Agent / producer
A properly authorized person who helps clients understand options, submits accurate applications and supports policy service.
Insured
The person whose life is covered.
Policyowner
The person or entity with ownership rights under the policy. The owner and insured can be different.
Beneficiary
The person or entity named to receive the death benefit. A contingent beneficiary is a backup if the primary beneficiary cannot receive it under the policy’s terms.

An IMO or FMO is an insurance distribution organization that may help agencies with carrier access, contracting or support. It does not replace the carrier’s underwriting authority.

3. Learn the language

Premium
The payment for the policy. A monthly premium of $100 means a scheduled $100 monthly payment.
Face amount / death benefit
The stated coverage amount and the benefit payable under the contract. Loans, riders and benefit options can affect the amount paid.
Underwriting
The insurer’s process for evaluating risk and deciding eligibility, classification and pricing.
Rider
An added policy provision that changes or adds benefits. Availability, charges and eligibility vary.
Cash value
A value within certain permanent policies. It is different from the death benefit and may differ from the amount available on surrender.
Effective date
The date coverage takes effect under the insurer’s requirements. Do not assume it is the application date.
In force
Coverage is active according to the insurer. Confirm outstanding requirements and payment status.
Lapse / surrender
A lapse is a loss of coverage under policy terms, often related to insufficient payment or value. Surrender is the owner ending the policy; any amount payable depends on its terms.

4. Understand the main products

Term life

Protection for a specified period. It generally has no cash value and often offers a lower initial cost than permanent coverage for the same death benefit. Review the level period, renewal pricing and conversion rules.

Whole life

A permanent policy with contractual guarantees when its required premiums and conditions are met. Review guaranteed values and distinguish them from any nonguaranteed dividends.

Universal life, including IUL

Permanent coverage with policy charges and funding requirements that need monitoring. Indexed universal life uses an index-linked crediting formula; it is not direct ownership of an index. Caps, participation rates, charges and guarantees depend on the contract. An illustration’s nonguaranteed values are projections, not promises.

Final expense and mortgage protection

These describe needs or marketing categories, not a single universal policy design. Final expense coverage commonly addresses funeral and other end-of-life costs. Mortgage protection aims to help with a mortgage obligation after a covered death. Always identify the actual product, beneficiary, exclusions and benefit schedule.

Simplified issue can still involve health questions and checks. Guaranteed issue does not mean every death receives the full benefit immediately. Read any graded benefit or waiting-period provisions.

Start with the client’s need, then compare eligible products. Ask a licensed supervisor when the product’s mechanics are outside your training.

5. Follow a deal from beginning to end

  1. Lead: A potential customer’s information, not a sale or proof of consent to every type of contact. Check contact restrictions and existing notes.
  2. Conversation: Identify yourself and your purpose. Find out whether the person wants help. Respect a request not to be contacted.
  3. Needs review: Discuss dependents, income, debts, existing coverage, budget and the time horizon.
  4. Eligibility and options: Obtain accurate answers and use the carrier’s current rules. Explain the recommendation and its tradeoffs.
  5. Application: Record the applicant’s own answers. Let them review the information and complete required consent and signatures through the approved process.
  6. Submitted: The insurer has received the application. Track additional questions or requirements.
  7. Decision and delivery: The insurer may approve, change the offer, postpone or decline. Explain the actual offer and complete delivery requirements.
  8. Coverage verification: Confirm the effective date, required payments and outstanding conditions with the insurer.
  9. Service: Help the client understand their policy, locate documents and maintain accurate contact and beneficiary information.

In our portal: A submitted deal, an agent-selected pipeline stage and the carrier’s recorded status serve different purposes. Moving a card to “In force” is not evidence that the insurer activated coverage.

6. How the money works

The client’s premium is not your paycheck. Commission depends on the carrier, product, eligible premium, your signed contract and whether payment conditions are met.

Illustrative example only:
$100 monthly premium × 12 = $1,200 annualized premium (AP).
If the contract pays 80% of that eligible amount: $1,200 × 80% = $960 first-year commission.
If 75% of that commission is advanced: $960 × 75% = $720 advanced. The remaining $240 is subject to the contract’s earning and payment rules.

This is teaching math, not a promise of your compensation. Actual contracts may use different commission bases, advances, exclusions, fees or schedules. An advance is money paid ahead of being fully earned. A chargeback is a contractual recovery of commission, which may occur when coverage ends or premiums are not paid. The amount and timing vary.

Renewal commissions, when provided, follow their own schedule. Agency overrides, when applicable, are governed by agency contracts. AP on a leaderboard measures production; it is not take-home income or carrier-confirmed commission.

Track actual deposits, expenses and potential chargebacks separately. Confirm your compensation schedule before estimating earnings.

7. A submitted application is the start of service

Persistency describes how well policies stay active over time. Focus on coverage the customer understands and can afford, accurate payment instructions and prompt follow-up.

  • Initial premium failed: Check the carrier’s instructions and contact the client appropriately. Do not tell them coverage is active without confirmation.
  • Grace period: Confirm the actual deadline and options in the contract; do not assume all carriers allow the same number of days.
  • Changed offer: Explain the revised terms and obtain required acceptance. Never substitute your own acceptance.
  • Cancellation or lapse: Record the carrier’s status, help with authorized service requests and review any commission adjustment.

Document the action, responsible agent and next follow-up date. Avoid pressure to retain coverage that does not meet the client’s needs.

8. What an agent does each day

  1. Review appointments, callbacks, policy requirements and urgent service tasks.
  2. Prepare: know the product, use current materials and check the lead’s notes and contact restrictions.
  3. Hold authorized conversations, listen carefully and record accurate outcomes.
  4. Follow up on applications and communicate carrier requests clearly.
  5. Update the CRM with the right client, product, premium, dates and next task.
  6. Review your work with your leader: conversations, appointments, applications, issued coverage and retention. Use the results to improve rather than promise yourself a fixed income.

A lead is not a guaranteed appointment. An appointment is not a guaranteed application. A submitted application is not a guaranteed issued or paid policy.

9. Before you sell

Complete the licensing and carrier authorization requirements for the states and products involved. In Michigan, selling, soliciting or negotiating insurance requires the appropriate producer license, and acting for an insurer requires appointment. Training or an NPN alone is not authorization. Unlicensed recruits should focus on study and approved non-sales activities.

  • Use truthful answers and current, approved product materials. Never invent health information, contact details or consent.
  • Never sign for the applicant or imply that you are a government agency, their lender or the insurer if that is untrue.
  • Keep sensitive client data in approved systems. Do not put banking details, identification documents or health records in general chat.
  • Honor do-not-call instructions and follow the agency’s approved contact and recording procedures.
  • Do not recommend replacing existing coverage casually. Review costs, lost benefits and new limitations; complete required replacement procedures.
  • Explain the policy’s review/cancellation period and claim process using the actual contract and applicable state requirements.

This orientation supplements your licensing course and supervised training. Product terms and applicable state requirements control.

10. Check your understanding

Explain each answer out loud before opening it.

A client submits an application today. Are they insured?

Not automatically. Confirm the carrier’s coverage conditions, effective date, payment and any temporary coverage agreement. Submission alone is not proof.

A policy costs $75 per month. Is that your commission?

No. Its annualized premium is $900. Commission depends on the eligible amount and the applicable contract. AP and agent income are different.

A client says the premium is too high. What should you do?

Revisit their need and sustainable budget. Discuss suitable alternatives within your authority, rather than pushing an unaffordable payment.

A client says “do not call,” but another record has a phone number. Can you use it?

Do not use another record to work around the request. Document it and follow the agency’s suppression and contact procedures.

What should you ask your leader next?

Which products am I trained and authorized to discuss? Where are my current carrier guides and compensation schedules? Who handles underwriting questions? How do I report contact restrictions and service issues?
