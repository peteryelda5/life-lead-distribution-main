Life Insurance 101 — Vivid Life Advisors

1. Purpose
Welcome to Life Insurance 101 from Vivid Life Advisors. This lesson is for someone starting from zero. Life insurance helps protect people financially when a covered death occurs. The customer pays premiums, and the insurance company agrees to pay a death benefit under the policy's terms. That money may help a family replace income, pay debts, or cover final expenses. Your job is to understand the client's situation and help them find suitable, affordable protection. Start by asking: Who depends on this person? What bills would remain? How long is coverage needed? And what payment can they maintain? A bigger policy is not automatically better. A quote is an estimate. An application is a request. Neither automatically means coverage has started. Keep those distinctions in mind throughout this lesson.

2. Know the people and the words
Let's define the people involved. The carrier is the insurance company. It evaluates applications, issues policies, collects premiums, and evaluates claims. Vivid Life Advisors is an agency, not the insurer. An agent, also called a producer, helps clients understand options and handles authorized sales and service work. The insured is the person whose life is covered. The policyowner has ownership rights. The beneficiary is named to receive the death benefit. The owner, insured, and beneficiary do not have to be the same person. A premium is the payment for the policy. Underwriting is the carrier's process for evaluating risk and deciding eligibility and price. A rider adds or changes benefits. The effective date is when coverage starts under the carrier's requirements. In force means coverage is active according to the insurer. An agency or an insurance marketing organization may support you, but it cannot override the insurer's underwriting decision.

3. Match the product to the need
Term life covers a specified period and generally does not build cash value. It can fit a temporary need, such as protection while children depend on a parent's income. Whole life is permanent coverage with contractual guarantees when required premiums and conditions are met. Distinguish guaranteed values from any nonguaranteed dividends. Universal life is another permanent design, with policy charges and funding requirements that need monitoring. Indexed universal life, or I U L, uses an index-linked crediting formula. It is not direct investment in an index, and illustrated nonguaranteed values are not promises. Final expense and mortgage protection describe needs or marketing categories, not one universal policy. Identify the actual product and its benefit schedule. Simplified issue can still involve health questions and checks. Guaranteed issue does not mean full benefits start immediately for every cause of death. Understand graded benefits, waiting periods, exclusions, and affordability before explaining an option.

4. From lead to active coverage
Here is the process. First, a lead is a potential customer's information, not a sale and not proof of consent to every type of contact. Check notes and contact restrictions. In an authorized conversation, review the person's needs, existing coverage, budget, and time horizon. Use accurate information and current carrier rules to compare eligible options. The applicant reviews their information and completes required consent and signatures through the approved process. After submission, the carrier may request more information, approve the application, change the offer, postpone it, or decline it. Explain the actual offer and complete delivery requirements. Before saying the client is covered, confirm the effective date, payment requirements, and outstanding conditions with the insurer. In our portal, a submitted deal, an agent's pipeline stage, and the carrier's status are different things. Moving a card to in force does not activate insurance. After coverage starts, continue helping the client with policy documents, questions, and authorized service.

5. AP is production—not your paycheck
Now let's separate the client's premium from your compensation. A hundred dollar monthly premium equals twelve hundred dollars in annualized premium, or A P. That is a production measure, not your income. Here is a fictional example. If a contract pays eighty percent of twelve hundred dollars of eligible premium, the first-year commission would be nine hundred sixty dollars. If seventy-five percent of that commission is advanced, the advance would be seven hundred twenty dollars. The remaining two hundred forty dollars depends on the contract's earning and payment rules. These are teaching numbers, not your guaranteed compensation. Actual products and contracts may use different bases, fees, schedules, or exclusions. An advance is money paid before it is fully earned. A chargeback is a contractual recovery of commission, which may happen when coverage ends or premiums are not paid. Renewal commissions and agency overrides follow their own contracts. Track actual deposits, business expenses, and potential chargebacks separately from leaderboard production.

6. Help clients keep suitable coverage
Submitting an application is not the end of your work. Persistency describes how well policies stay active over time. It starts with coverage that fits the client's need and a payment they can maintain. If an initial premium fails, follow the carrier's instructions and contact the client appropriately. Do not assume coverage is active. A grace period is an opportunity defined by the policy and applicable requirements; confirm the actual deadline rather than assuming every carrier uses the same number of days. If the insurer changes the offer, explain the new terms and obtain required acceptance. Never accept on the client's behalf. If coverage lapses or the client cancels, record the insurer's status, help with authorized service requests, and review any commission adjustment. Document the action, who is responsible, and the next follow-up date. Good service does not mean pressuring someone to keep unsuitable coverage. It means helping them make an informed decision and keeping accurate records.

7. Build good habits from day one
A useful daily routine starts with appointments, callbacks, carrier requirements, and urgent service tasks. Prepare with current product materials. Check the lead's notes and contact restrictions. Hold authorized conversations, listen carefully, record accurate outcomes, and schedule the next action. Review your results with your leader to improve your work. Before selling, complete the licensing and carrier authorization requirements for the relevant states and products. In Michigan, selling, soliciting, or negotiating insurance requires the appropriate producer license, and acting for an insurer requires appointment. Training and an N P N alone are not authorization. Never invent health answers, contact details, or consent. Never sign for the applicant. Use approved systems for private client information. Respect a do-not-call request; do not use another duplicate record to work around it. Do not replace existing coverage casually. Review lost benefits, costs, and new limitations through required procedures. When unsure, ask a qualified supervisor before proceeding.

8. Put it together
Let's put it together with a fictional client. The client wants protection for their family, has a limited monthly budget, and already has some coverage. You start with their needs and existing policy, not with a promise of a new sale. A properly authorized agent compares suitable options and explains the actual terms. The client gives accurate answers and completes the application process. The carrier makes its decision. You then verify coverage conditions and help with service. If the client pays seventy-five dollars monthly, the annualized premium is nine hundred dollars. That still does not tell you your commission without the applicable contract. You are ready for the next step when you can explain a premium, a beneficiary, term versus permanent coverage, and why submission is not the same as active coverage.
